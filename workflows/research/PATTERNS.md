# Research room patterns

These patterns describe a reusable way to organise research work. Adapt them to the field, project, methods and level of risk. They are prompts for good practice, not a prescribed methodology or a substitute for discipline-specific standards.

For a step-by-step explanation and copyable records, start with [`PRACTICES.md`](PRACTICES.md), then use the fictional [worked example](examples/search-strategy-evaluation.md) and the [project templates](templates/).

## 1. Frame the question before building

- State the research question in a form that can be answered with evidence.
- Say what observation or result would weaken, refute or change the working claim.
- Identify the unit being studied, the comparison or interpretation being made, and the main plausible sources of bias.
- Choose the kind of evidence and method because they fit the question; do not let the available tool define the question.
- Sketch the output a reader will inspect: for example, a comparison table, figure, proof, synthesis, coding scheme or system demonstration.

For exploratory work, label it exploratory and treat new claims as candidates. Before confirmatory or claim-bearing work, record the design and decision criteria in a dated protocol or equivalent project record.

## 2. Build an evidence trail

- Keep a source list or evidence ledger with stable identifiers, citations and links from claims to supporting material.
- Separate a source's reported finding from your interpretation of it.
- Record uncertainty, disagreement, exclusions and evidence that does not support the preferred account.
- Use a citation check before circulating academic claims or a literature synthesis.

Store source material and project-specific notes in the relevant project repository or approved storage. This public template should contain no private corpus or personal research records.

## 3. Make work reproducible

Before analysis or implementation, record enough to understand and, where permitted, repeat the work: inputs and their versions, inclusion rules, code or method version, environment, random seeds where relevant, analysis order and output locations. Keep a dated change log when the plan changes after work begins; preserve the original plan and explain material deviations.

## 4. Keep execution and findings distinct

Track whether the work was ready, run, validated and independently reviewed separately from what the evidence says. A successful run does not establish a hypothesis; a failed run does not establish the opposite. Preserve null, negative, inconclusive and non-estimable outcomes, and state the limits of each conclusion.

For costly, sensitive or externally consequential work, define the smallest useful check, resource limits, data-handling boundary and human approval points before execution. Scale the verification effort to the consequences of error.

## 5. Link communication back to evidence

For each important result shared in a paper, report, presentation or decision, keep a route back to its source evidence and calculation or analysis. Review whether the wording matches the strength and scope of that evidence, and whether the intended audience can follow the reasoning.

## 6. Keep room state maintainable

- `CURRENT.md` describes active work and next steps; update it as work changes.
- `knowledge/` holds durable, reusable knowledge, with citations and provenance where relevant.
- Project repositories hold project code, data, protocols and detailed records.
- This workspace repository holds shared room structure and generic procedures, not a second copy of project state.

See `CONTEXT.md` for the room remit and `../../SOURCE_OF_TRUTH.md` for the workspace ownership map.
