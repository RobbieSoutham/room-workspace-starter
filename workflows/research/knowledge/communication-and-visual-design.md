# Research communication and visual design

**Owner:** Research room · **Status:** Canonical source of truth for general research presentation and visual-communication practice.

Adapt these patterns to the audience, medium, field and required reporting standards. They support clear communication; they do not change what the evidence can establish.

## Structure for the audience

- Decide what the audience needs to understand or do. Organise the narrative around that purpose and finish with the implication, decision or next question when one is needed.
- Give each slide, figure or section one main job. Use claim-led headings when the content supports a conclusion; use neutral labels when the evidence is exploratory or the audience needs orientation.
- Explain a complex workflow, method, evaluation or provenance path with a simple diagram before adding dense prose or tables.
- Put essential context before results: define the question, cases or units, method, comparison and evidence object clearly enough for the audience to interpret the result.
- Use speaker notes as delivery cues. Move detail that is not needed for the main narrative to a clearly labelled appendix or backup section.

## Keep evidence and interpretation distinct

- Distinguish source-supported literature claims, results from the current project, interpretation and planned work. Label uncertainty and incomplete work clearly.
- Verify a paper's method, comparison, result, venue and limitations from the paper or authoritative record before characterising it. Do not rely on memory or an abstract for a precise claim.
- Keep citations close to claim-bearing content and provide a complete reference list appropriate to the discipline and format.
- Represent the work that actually occurred. A deterministic trace with model decisions is not automatically an autonomous trajectory; label the method and level of automation accurately.
- For complex evaluations, pair aggregate summaries with an inspectable example when it helps readers understand how the result was produced.

## Make visuals readable and truthful

- Use clear boundaries for inputs, processing, review, governance and outputs. Align related elements, use consistent spacing and line geometry, and route connections so they do not obscure labels or imply relationships that were not tested.
- Use colour and line style consistently. Prefer role-based encoding where colour must distinguish types of elements; label meanings so the figure remains interpretable without colour alone.
- Wrap long labels deliberately and inspect at the final viewing size. Increase figure area, simplify or split panels before shrinking text until it is difficult to read.
- Choose chart scales and panel layouts that preserve meaningful differences. Use separate panels when a shared scale would hide important variation; state scale changes clearly.
- Show missing or unavailable observations explicitly. Do not interpolate, omit or visually imply a value that was not observed.
- Name assets for their meaning rather than their temporary position in a draft. Render with a tool that supports the visual features used, then inspect the exported artifact rather than trusting the source file alone.

## Maintenance

Use the source-of-truth documents for evidence, research integrity and claim boundaries. Update this document when a communication or visual pattern proves reusable across projects; keep renderer-specific fixes with the tool that owns them and project-specific examples in the project repository.
